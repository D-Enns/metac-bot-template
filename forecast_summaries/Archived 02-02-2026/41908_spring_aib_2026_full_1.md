The World Health Organization (WHO) is hosting a webinar series under the WHO Information Network for Epidemics (EPI-WIN) to enhance the technical capacity of port health officers and public health inspectors in the WHO African, Eastern Mediterranean, and European Regions. The webinar focuses on conducting public health ship inspections and issuing ship sanitation certificates (SSCs) in accordance with the International Health Regulations (IHR) (2005), particularly as amended and effective from 19 September 2025. **WHO chief calls 2025 a year of 'stark contrasts' as landmark health deals met funding cuts**
WHO Director-General Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus described 2025 as a year of 'stark contrasts' for the World Health Organization, marked by landmark global health achievements—including the adoption of the Pandemic Agreement, the entry into force of amended International Health Regulations, approval of an increase in assessed contributions, and the adoption of a UN political declaration on noncommunicable diseases and mental health—while simultaneously facing severe financial strain. Due to significant funding cuts, WHO was forced to reduce its workforce, making 2025 'one of the most difficult years in our Organization's history.' Tedros highlighted that these challenges are part of a broader crisis affecting international organizations, driven by sudden and severe cuts to bilateral aid. He emphasized that WHO has mobilized 85% of the resources needed for its base budget through assessed contribution increases, with more increases scheduled through 2031. However, he warned that the remaining funding gap would be difficult to close, and earmarked funding continues to create 'pockets of poverty' in critical areas such as emergency preparedness, antimicrobial resistance (AMR), health financing, climate resilience, and determinants of health. Tedros stressed that failure to approve assessed contribution increases would have left WHO in a far worse situation, and that continued reforms are essential to ensure an impartial, science-based organization. 'The story of 2025 is not one of austerity, but resolve,' he said. 'This is your WHO. Its strength is your unity. Its future is your choice.'

**What Have We Learned from the Pandemic? Lessons from WHO Epidemiologist Maria Van Kerkhove**
Six years after the World Health Organization (WHO) declared the Sars-CoV-2 outbreak a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) on January 30, 2020, epidemiologist Maria Van Kerkhove, interim director of the WHO's Epidemic and Pandemic Threat Management Department, reflects on the lessons learned from the pandemic. She emphasizes that while the world witnessed the rapid spread of a novel virus, it also demonstrated the immense power of science, collaboration, and data sharing when aligned. However, inequalities significantly slowed progress and caused preventable loss of life. Van Kerkhove notes that the pandemic’s end in 2023 did not mark the end of the threat—Sars-CoV-2 continues to circulate globally, evolve, and cause severe illness and Long Covid. She highlights that effective tools now exist, including updated vaccines, treatments, surveillance systems, and preventive measures, but uptake remains insufficient, especially among high-risk populations. The WHO’s updated strategy focuses on protection, integration, and preparedness, integrating Covid-19 care into routine health systems. Van Kerkhove stresses the importance of maintaining surveillance and data sharing, warning that reduced testing and reporting have created critical data gaps. She underscores the growing burden of Long Covid, affecting an estimated 10–20% of individuals after initial recovery. She also honors healthcare workers, scientists, and first responders who endured immense personal and professional strain. A haunting image—a patient’s hand held between two rubber gloves filled with warm water to simulate touch—symbolizes the emotional toll of isolation. Her message for 2026: protect vulnerable populations, maintain rigorous surveillance, invest in Long Covid research and care, and prepare for future crises proactively, not reactively.

**Ministry of Health Approves First Permanent Guidelines for COVID-19 Treatment**
The Russian Ministry of Health has approved the first permanent clinical guidelines for the treatment of COVID-19. According to the document, infectious sources include not only symptomatic individuals but also those in the incubation period and asymptomatic carriers of SARS-CoV-2. The highest risk to others occurs in the final stage of the incubation period and the first days of illness. The virus is classified as a pathogen of the second group, with an average incubation period of 3–4 days. Outpatient treatment includes antiviral drugs such as molnupiravir, favipiravir, nirmatrelvir with ritonavir, and other active substances. Medications with indirect antiviral effects and immunostimulants are also permitted, depending on clinical presentation. Home-based medical care is recommended for adults with positive COVID-19 tests who have no symptoms or mild disease. The guidelines note that hypoxia occurs in about one-third of infected individuals, and asymptomatic cases may contribute to hidden transmission. The document emphasizes evaluating comorbidities and conditions such as pregnancy to identify risk factors for severe or complicated disease. Recommendations also cover post-recovery rehabilitation and prevention, including vaccination: vector vaccines are advised for patients with oncological diseases and people living with HIV, regardless of treatment regimen or immune status. The document references that Rospotrebnadzor found no new 'terrifying' mutations of the virus. The outbreak was first reported in Wuhan, China, in January 2020, and the virus was identified as SARS-CoV-2 by January 9. The WHO declared the outbreak a Public Health Emergency of International Concern on January 30, 2020, and Russia reported its first cases the following day. Global death toll estimates range from 6.9 million officially recorded to up to 20 million according to WHO. The WHO officially ended the pandemic status on May 5, 2023, after a duration of three years, one month, and 24 days, noting that the virus remains a public health threat.
