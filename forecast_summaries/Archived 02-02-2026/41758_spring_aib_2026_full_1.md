**US, Russia threats to resume nuclear tests raise global security fears**
The United States and Russia have both recently threatened to resume nuclear testing, raising global security concerns and undermining the international norm against such tests. US President Donald Trump announced on Truth Social in late October that he had instructed the Department of War to begin nuclear testing on an equal basis, though the White House clarified that the tests would not involve nuclear explosions. Russia’s President Vladimir Putin responded by stating that if the US or any other signatory to the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty (CTBT) conducts nuclear tests, Russia would be obligated to take reciprocal measures. The CTBT, adopted by the UN General Assembly in 1996, has been signed by 187 states and ratified by 178, but it has not entered into force because nine key states— including the US, China, Egypt, Iran, Israel, India, North Korea, and Pakistan—have not ratified it. Russia revoked its ratification in 2023, citing an imbalance with the US failure to ratify. The CTBT Organization, based in Vienna, operates a global monitoring network of 307 stations using seismic, hydroacoustic, infrasound, and radionuclide technologies, with a 2025 budget exceeding USD 139 million. It successfully detected all six North Korean nuclear tests between 2006 and 2017. Experts such as Daryl Kimball of the Arms Control Association warn that resuming tests could destabilize global security and encourage countries like China and India to conduct full-scale tests to refine smaller warhead designs. Joseph Rodgers of CSIS notes that while the US and Russia have conducted most historical tests, countries with less experience may benefit more from resuming testing. Subcritical experiments, which do not produce explosions or self-sustaining chain reactions, are permitted under the treaty and are routinely conducted by nuclear-armed states. However, Kimball highlights a verification gap: hydronuclear tests with extremely low yields in underground metal chambers may go undetected, as the monitoring system was designed to detect explosions of at least 1 kiloton, though it can detect down to 500 tons of TNT. The Hiroshima bomb had a yield of about 15 kilotons. The CTBT Organization’s Executive Secretary, Robert Floyd, emphasized that the organization’s role is to provide confidence that any nuclear explosion would be detected, regardless of yield.
