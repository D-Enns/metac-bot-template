**Skinny Facts May Limit Supreme Court's Decision in 'Skinny Label' Generic Drug Approval Case**
The U.S. Supreme Court agreed to hear Hikma Pharmaceuticals USA Inc. v. Amarin Pharma, Inc. on January 16, 2026, to determine the limits of induced patent infringement claims against generic drug manufacturers using 'skinny labels'—labels that omit patent-protected indications. Under the Hatch-Waxman Act, generic companies can file an abbreviated new drug application (ANDA) and use a Section viii statement to exclude patented uses from their labeling, provided the FDA approves the label as safe and effective. However, this pathway does not shield generics from induced infringement claims. In this case, Amarin sued Hikma, alleging that Hikma induced infringement of its method-of-use patents for the cardiovascular (CV) indication of Vascepa by marketing its generic version as a 'generic equivalent' in press releases, investor communications, and a website listing the product under 'Hypertriglyceridemia' with an AB rating and a disclaimer about limited approvals. The district court dismissed the claim, but the Federal Circuit reversed, holding that the allegations plausibly pleaded induced infringement. The Supreme Court granted certiorari, with the U.S. Solicitor General supporting Hikma's petition. The Court will only rule on whether such statements, if proven, are sufficient to constitute induced infringement at the pleading stage and will not address pre-launch scenarios or broader labeling carve-out issues. As a result, the decision is expected to have limited impact, likely clarifying rather than overruling the Federal Circuit's approach. Generic sponsors should anticipate continued litigation over promotional practices despite the ruling.

**Digital Health Policy- FDA Relaxes Restrictions over Wearables and AI Decision Making Tools in Two New 2026 Guidances**
On January 6, 2026, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) released updated guidance documents—General Wellness: Policy for Low Risk Devices and Clinical Decision Support Software (CDS Guidance)—which supersede earlier versions from 2019 and 2022. The updates reflect a regulatory shift toward relaxing restrictions on low-risk digital health technologies, including wearables and AI-powered 'chatbots.' The General Wellness Guidance clarifies that non-invasive devices estimating physiologic parameters (e.g., blood pressure, glucose, heart rate variability) may be marketed as wellness products under certain conditions, provided they make only wellness claims (e.g., sleep, stress, activity) and not medical claims (e.g., diagnosis, monitoring, or management of disease). The guidance also outlines criteria for notification features, such as alerts suggesting medical evaluation, which can remain within the wellness category if they meet specified thresholds. The CDS Guidance introduces enforcement discretion for clinical decision support tools that provide a single clinically appropriate treatment recommendation based on inputs like patient diagnosis, comorbidities, and preferences—provided a healthcare provider reviews, revises, and finalizes the plan. However, if the software analyzes unique diagnostic images (e.g., PET scans), it falls outside enforcement discretion due to the generation of specific medical directives. The FDA issued these non-binding guidances without a notice-and-comment period, allowing immediate implementation. According to FDA Commissioner Martin Makary, the move aims to provide clarity, market predictability, and investor confidence. The shift underscores FDA’s focus on fostering innovation in digital health while emphasizing the need for manufacturers to avoid overstating product capabilities or discouraging professional medical care. The changes align with broader federal goals to strengthen the U.S. medtech innovation ecosystem.
