Research Summary
The collected reporting documents an era of unprecedented AI acceleration across adoption, compute scaling and benchmark performance, while simultaneously underscoring the limitations of current evaluation methods and the difficulty of forecasting. Industry metrics show frontier models achieving high scores on established tests—such as AI reaching 87.8 % on the MATH dataset by April 2024 and near-perfect accuracy on some math contests via inference-time systems like DeepConf (99.9 % on AIME 2025)—and companies like Blitzy reporting an 86.8 % result on the SWE-bench Verified coding benchmark. Yet multiple sources warn that many benchmarks can be gamed or suffer from data leaks (e.g., Meta’s SWE-bench loopholes and OpenAI’s FrontierMath dataset access), leading to inflated scores. Studies of forecasting tournaments and expert judgments reveal systematic underestimation of AI progress: domain experts and superforecasters assigned low probabilities (often below 25 %) to milestones that models then surpassed years ahead of schedule, compressing expected timelines for advanced capabilities.
At the same time, analyses caution that benchmark excellence does not guarantee real-world effectiveness or trustworthy deployment. Goodhart’s law—in which measures become targets and lose validity—appears to apply when models are tuned specifically for test performance. Proposals for more rigorous evaluation ecosystems include metrology-inspired frameworks, live-event prediction platforms (like Prophet Arena) where AI matches market forecasts, and new, harder benchmarks (ZeroBench, EnigmaEval) designed to resist saturation and cheating. This body of research thus frames AI’s rapid ascent in both technical prowess and hype, highlights persistent uncertainties in evaluation, and emphasizes the challenge of predicting whether future benchmarks—such as achieving over 85 % on FrontierMath before 2026—will reliably reflect meaningful progress.

Chatbots Are Spreading More False Claims, NewsGuard Report Shows
The NewsGuard audit of the ten most widely used AI chatbots found that their accuracy on news‑related questions has fallen sharply. In August 2025, the tools repeated false claims in 35 percent of responses, up from 18 percent a year earlier, a change the report links to new system designs that eliminated the refusal rate (which was 31 percent in 2024 and fell to zero in 2025). The audit tested OpenAI, Google, Microsoft, Meta, Anthropic, Mistral, Inflection, xAI, Perplexity and You.com using prompts built from known false‑claim fingerprints across politics, health, international affairs and corporate topics. Inflection’s Pi chatbot performed worst, repeating false claims 56.7 percent of the time; Perplexity’s failure rate rose from 0 percent in 2024 to 46.7 percent in 2025; ChatGPT and Meta each scored 40 percent; Microsoft’s Copilot and Mistral’s Le Chat were at 36.7 percent; Claude was the most accurate, with false claims in 10 percent of responses; and Google’s Gemini recorded 16.7 percent. The report notes that many false statements entered chatbot outputs via propaganda networks and low‑quality online content, citing Russian‑linked groups such as Storm‑1516 and the Pravda network that seeded fabricated stories about Moldovan parliamentary leader Igor Grosu, which six of the ten chatbots repeated as fact. Despite developers’ safety announcements—OpenAI’s GPT‑5, Google’s Gemini 2.5, and Mistral’s media‑partner strategy—the audit shows limited progress, with Mistral’s Le Chat maintaining a 36.7 percent failure rate in both 2024 and 2025. The study warns that refusing to answer questions leaves users uninformed, while answering every question without careful sourcing creates a false sense of safety: 'The current approach of always providing an answer, even when drawn from unreliable sources, creates a different false sense of safety by offering confident replies that repeat falsehoods,' the report cautions. The risk, the report states, is not a single false claim but long‑term exposure to misinformation that blurs fact from fiction.
