Research Summary
Research across domestic and international outlets shows that, with federal appropriations expiring on September 30, 2025, Congress had not agreed on a continuing resolution by late September, making a government shutdown on October 1 increasingly likely. Republicans control both chambers and the presidency but lack the 60‐vote Senate supermajority needed to overcome a filibuster, forcing them to seek Democratic backing. The GOP-crafted stopgap would extend funding through November 21 and add security funds, while Democrats insisted on reversing Medicaid cuts and extending Affordable Care Act subsidies through October 31. Both measures failed in the Senate. Analysts at Wolfe Research and Bank of America place shutdown odds above 65%–“very likely,” noting that neither party has budged, and President Trump publicly declined to concede, cancelling talks with Democratic leaders and suggesting he would allow a shutdown rather than compromise.
Contingency plans at agencies like the Bureau of Labor Statistics would halt data collection and furlough roughly 2,000 employees, delaying critical economic reports (non-farm payrolls, CPI) and complicating Federal Reserve policymaking. An Office of Management and Budget memo instructs agencies to draft permanent reduction-in-force notices for programs not aligned with presidential priorities, marking a shift from prior shutdowns’ temporary furloughs. Historical analysis indicates even brief closures erode morale—employees furloughed in 2013 were 31% likelier to quit within a year—and spur higher contractor spending and long-term productivity losses. Although essential services (Social Security, air-traffic control, the military) continue, non-essential operations (national parks, museums, visa processing) would suspend, and the Congressional Budget Office estimates each week of shutdown shaves roughly 0.1 percentage point off GDP.

Even a brief government shutdown might hamper morale, raise costs and reduce long-term efficiency in the federal workforce
An analysis of a potential October 2025 federal shutdown shows that even a brief shutdown can have lasting negative effects on the federal workforce. The article notes that the current fiscal year is ending and that Democrats and Republicans rely on short‑term funding bills; with no agreement, the government would shut down on Oct. 1, 2025. The federal workforce has already shed more than 300,000 jobs in 2025 due to Trump‑era downsizing. A shutdown would furlough hundreds of thousands of employees, who would receive back pay but face short‑term financial strain. Using administrative records from the October 2013 shutdown, the authors found that 'Employees exposed to furloughs were 31 % more likely to leave their jobs within one year', and that the exodus continued over the first two years, leaving a permanently lower headcount. The loss was greatest among young, female and highly educated workers. The authors estimate that 'the drop in intrinsic motivation would require a roughly 10 % wage increase to offset'. Agencies that were hardest hit turned to contractors, spending about US$1 billion more on temporary staff than they saved in payroll. Performance losses included higher rates of inaccurate federal payments, lower legal enforcement and reduced patenting activity. The article quotes Gonzalo Maturana, Andrew Teodorescu and Christoph Herpfer as authors. The authors conclude that shutdowns are blunt shocks that erode morale and productivity, and that the long‑term costs outweigh the short‑term savings.

US Government Shutdown: Modest Impact but New Blow to Credibility
A United States government shutdown is looming, but markets remain largely calm. According to Bank of America, the shutdown would be modest even if it starts next week without any approved funding measures, and the Congress has yet to pass a single budget allocation, making a full shutdown more likely than a partial one. Market analysts see a probability above 65% for a shutdown, yet historically such events have had a small impact: previous shutdowns reduced GDP by only 0.1 percentage point per week, with growth usually recovering as furloughed employees are later paid. Essential services—air traffic control, Social Security—continue to operate. The main concern is a freeze on economic data; a prolonged shutdown would force the Federal Reserve to rely on private research just weeks before its October meeting, increasing policy uncertainty. The White House has directed agencies to prepare for permanent staff reductions rather than temporary furloughs, which could hurt Washington’s already weaker local labor market and make it harder to recover growth if layoffs persist. Markets are unlikely to retreat significantly; Treasury yields and the dollar have barely moved in past shutdowns, and the 2018 decline was more due to Fed tightening than a budget impasse. Bank of America notes that risk‑seeking assets, rates, and currency usually weather shutdowns calmly, and the monetary market system is unaffected. Unlike debt‑ceiling battles, there is no default threat. However, BofA analysts say the biggest risk is political: another budget impasse could reinforce the perception of a dysfunctional fiscal process. Credit rating agencies have previously downgraded the United States over debt‑limit disputes, though never because of a shutdown. If investors conclude that Washington’s fiscal paralysis is chronic, the rating could change. For now, a government funding interruption is more an inconvenience than a crisis, but it erodes confidence in America’s governing capacity.
