Cameroon's youth stand at a crossroads ahead of the presidential election
Cameroon's youth, who make up more than 60% of the population, are at a crossroads as the presidential election approaches on 12 October 2025. Officially, the youth unemployment rate is 6.23% in 2024, yet many young people still struggle to find stable work. Youth voices express a mix of hope and frustration. Analysts note that while young voters could end decades of single‑party rule if mobilised, they are "not yet up to the level of coordinated action that is independent of politics," according to political analyst Ngala Desmond. The incumbent president, Paul Biya, 93, has been in power since 1982 and announced in July that he would seek re‑election despite health rumors. The election therefore presents a pivotal moment for the country’s future.

The article reports that Cameroon is scheduled to hold a presidential election in less than a month. The incumbent, 92‑year‑old President Paul Biya, is seeking an eighth term and has been in power since 1982. He faces eleven other candidates, including Issa Tchiroma Bakary, a former minister who resigned in June, and Maurice Kamto, Biya’s main opponent whose candidacy was rejected last month. The election will take place amid a tense security environment: Islamist militants remain active in the Far North, while separatist forces in the Anglophone Northwest and Southwest regions continue to fight for independence as the self‑declared state of ‘Ambazonia’. Separatists have a history of boycotting elections, describing them as ‘illegitimate’ and ‘foreign’, and have previously called for lockdowns, civilian stay‑at‑home orders, and attacks on polling stations, which led to low voter turnout in 2018 and 2020. The government urges civilians to vote while soldiers are deployed for security. The International Crisis Group (ICG) has called for an ‘electoral ceasefire’ between the government and separatists. Since the beginning of the month, violence has escalated: on September 5, eight soldiers were killed in an IED attack in Malende, a village near Buea, claimed by the Fako Unity Warriors; the next day a gendarme was killed in Wututu, near Limbe, also claimed by the same militia. Nine soldiers were killed in separate attacks within 24 hours. Since September 8, separatists have imposed a one‑month lockdown to disrupt school resumption and the upcoming election. These incidents have raised fears of an upsurge of violence during the presidential election.
