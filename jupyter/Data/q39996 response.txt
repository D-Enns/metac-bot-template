The collected reporting focuses on Congress’s efforts in September 2025 to avert a partial federal shutdown on October 1 by passing short-term continuing resolutions (CRs). On September 17–19, the Republican-controlled House approved a CR to fund agencies at current levels through November 21, with provisions for enhanced security funding, but final enactment depended on securing 60 votes in the Senate. Senate Democrats blocked the measure, citing partisan objections and demands to protect expiring health-subsidy programs, while the White House and President Trump publicly urged passage “without modifications.” Separate coverage notes that Rep. Ryan Zinke supported a “clean” CR through mid-November, and the White House proposed extending funding via a CR to January 31, 2026, emphasizing the risk to hundreds of thousands of federal employees and critical services if Congress failed to act.
In parallel, the Senate confirmed 48 of President Trump’s nominees for diplomatic, military, and other posts in a single 51–47 block vote enabled by a new majority-friendly rule, installing figures such as Kimberly Guilfoyle as ambassador to Greece and Callista Gingrich to Switzerland and Liechtenstein. On the executive side, two presidential memoranda imposed a hiring freeze on new Federal civilian positions—first through January 20, 2025, and later extended to October 15, 2025—while exempting national security, public safety, and certain benefit-delivery roles and directing OPM and OMB to develop workforce-reduction plans. No articles in the set report any incumbent U.S. federal officials resigning or otherwise leaving their posts before October 1, 2025.

U.S. House Advances Temporary Funding Bill to Avoid Government Shutdown
On September 17, 2025, the U.S. House of Representatives, with a Republican majority, passed a procedural resolution 216‑210 that authorizes debate on a continuing resolution (CR) to fund federal agencies through November 21 and give both chambers additional time to agree on a full 2026 fiscal‑year appropriations bill. The CR includes $88 million for security for members of Congress, the Supreme Court and the executive branch. House Republicans aim to pass the CR by Friday, send it to the Senate, and obtain the President’s signature. The White House has endorsed the measure, while Democrats oppose it, calling it a “party‑polarized Republican bill.” On the same night, Democrats introduced a competing CR that would keep the government open until October 31, restore Medicaid funding for low‑income Americans (which had been cut by the Trump tax‑reform bill), and permanently extend the Affordable Care Act subsidies. Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer said, 'We still have two weeks. They should sit down with us and maybe we can reach a good proposal. Let’s wait and see.' He contrasted the parties’ priorities, noting that Republicans want to maintain the status quo while Democrats seek to improve healthcare and lower costs. A Senate Republican aide described the Democratic proposal as “partisan” and “not a serious proposal.” Republicans argue their CR will streamline government and spur economic growth, warning that without it most families will soon face higher taxes. The Senate, which holds a 53‑47 majority, would need 60 votes to pass the CR by the October 1 deadline, requiring at least seven Democrats to join the Republicans. The annual appropriations debate covers roughly a quarter of the $7 trillion federal budget, excluding mandatory programs such as Social Security, Medicare and interest on the $37.5 trillion national debt.

Hiring Freeze
The President, exercising constitutional authority, issued a memorandum ordering a freeze on the hiring of Federal civilian employees across the executive branch. The order states that 'no Federal civilian position that is vacant at noon on January 20, 2025, may be filled' and that 'no new position may be created except as otherwise provided for in this memorandum or other applicable law.' The freeze does not apply to military personnel, immigration enforcement, national security, public safety, or to the provision of Social Security, Medicare, or Veterans benefits. The Office of Personnel Management may grant exemptions where necessary. Within 90 days, the Office of Management and Budget, in consultation with OPM and the USDS, must submit a plan to reduce the Federal workforce through efficiency and attrition; once this plan is issued, the freeze expires for all agencies except the Internal Revenue Service, which remains frozen until the Treasury Secretary decides otherwise. The memorandum prohibits contracting outside the Federal Government to circumvent its intent, allows heads of agencies to reallocate existing personnel and funds for essential services, and explicitly does not limit presidential appointments, Senate confirmations, or collective bargaining agreements.

White House Pushes for Continuing Resolution to Avoid Government Shutdown
The White House is urging Congress to pass a continuing‑resolution (CR) that would extend federal funding to January 31 2026 to avoid a partial shutdown beginning October 1 2025.  House Majority Leader Steve Scalise said the proposal is "仅为一项建议" and that the exact dates and schedule are not yet fixed.  The CR would keep current spending levels but could add emergency accounts such as FEMA disaster relief.  Federal funding expires on September 30 2025; without a new CR, agencies would shut down and hundreds of thousands of federal employees would be placed on unpaid leave.  Congress has not yet agreed on the 12 discretionary‑spending bills that total about $1.6 trillion, roughly 7 trillion of the total federal budget according to the Congressional Budget Office.  Debt interest now exceeds defense spending, and the national debt stands at $37.4 trillion, raising fiscal concerns.  Democratic leaders Rosa DeLauro and Patty Murray have called the White House request "极端" and cited the Trump administration’s use of executive power to freeze more than $412 billion of approved spending, deepening partisan tensions.  The Office of Personnel Management announced plans to cut about 300,000 federal employees through layoffs, early retirement, voluntary resignation, and buy‑outs, a reduction of roughly 12.5 % from the beginning of the year.  Republicans hold a 219‑212 majority in the House and a 53‑47 lead in the Senate, but a CR would still require at least seven Democratic votes to reach the 60‑vote threshold.  If Congress fails to act before September 30, a partial shutdown would begin on October 1, shutting non‑essential departments and leaving many employees unpaid.  Since 1981, the United States has experienced 14 partial shutdowns; the most recent, from December 2018 to January 2019, lasted 35 days, the longest in U.S. history.
