Earlier reviews confirmed that deficiencies in combating money laundering and terrorist financing had been addressed in principle, but inspectors will verify implementation and sustainability. Multiple analyses suggest that a positive October verdict would lead to delisting at the FATF plenary, likely in October 2025 or early 2026, which is expected to enhance investor confidence, reduce financial-sector compliance costs and facilitate smoother capital flows.

Govt taking deliberate steps to restore fiscal discipline, stabilise debt: Masondo
Deputy Finance Minister David Masondo told investors at the RMB Morgan Stanley Investor Conference that the government is taking 'deliberate steps' to restore fiscal discipline and stabilise debt. He said the debt‑to‑GDP ratio will stabilise at 77.4 % this financial year and fall to 74.4 % by 2029/30. Masondo highlighted that South Africa has run a primary budget surplus for three consecutive years, which helps reduce debt‑service costs and free up resources for growth‑enhancing investments. He projected the gross borrowing requirement to decline to R434 billion in 2026/27 before rising to R588 billion in 2027/28, and that the Treasury will raise $14.6 billion to meet foreign‑exchange commitments over the medium term. Masondo also confirmed plans to diversify borrowing instruments, including green bonds and sukuk, and to use a balanced mix of domestic and foreign borrowing. Fitch Ratings noted that the country’s debt rose to 78.1 % of GDP in 2024 and is expected to reach nearly 80 % by 2027, far above the peer median of 54 %. Fitch warned that fiscal flexibility is hampered by a rigid expenditure structure, with wages and interest payments consuming almost half of government spending. The agency projects South Africa’s economy will grow only 1.2 % annually between 2025 and 2027, below the 3.7 % median growth forecast for peers. Masondo stressed that growth is critical for tax revenue, job creation, and corporate earnings, and cited the Metro Trading Services Reform under Operation Vulindlela Phase II as a structural reform that could unlock at least R108 billion in additional municipal infrastructure investment. He concluded that the government is serious about fiscal sustainability, implementing reforms to unlock growth, and inviting investors to partner on this journey.

BUDGET 2024 - an overview - bbrief
The South African Treasury released a detailed overview of the 2024/25 budget, highlighting a mix of fiscal tightening and targeted spending. The consolidated budget deficit is projected at 4.5% of GDP, which is lower than the Reuters market consensus of 5% and better than the Treasury’s own medium‑term target of 5.2%. The Treasury also expects a primary surplus that will grow to 1.8% of GDP over the medium term, signalling a commitment to debt‑stabilising fiscal policy. Debt per person is estimated at R82,711, with the gross debt ratio projected to peak at 75.3% of GDP by FY 2025/26, about 2% lower than previously forecast.
Revenue is expected to rise by R0.7 billion in the current fiscal year relative to the medium‑term estimates, but will still be R56.1 billion lower than the previous year, largely due to an undershoot in corporate income taxes and VAT. Personal income taxes performed well, helped by nominal wage gains. The Treasury introduced modest tax measures: sin taxes were increased by roughly 7% for alcohol and 6.5% for tobacco, while no further fuel tax adjustments were made.
On the expenditure side, the Treasury cut the spending ceiling by R21 billion for FY 2024/25 and by R28.1 billion for FY 2025/26, even as it allocated an additional R57.6 billion over the medium term to honour the 2023 public‑service wage agreement. The civil‑service wage bill will still account for 38.3% of non‑interest spending by FY 2026/27, with real growth in the wage bill expected to decline by an average of 0.1% per annum. Health spending will decline at an average of 1.2% per year, and the Treasury did not allocate further bailout funds for state‑owned entities.
Social relief measures were extended: the Social Relief of Distress grant will receive R35.2 billion for FY 2025/26 and R36.8 billion for FY 2026/27. Pension and child grants are projected to rise by 0.1% and 0.3% in real terms, respectively. The Treasury also announced a new funding mechanism to curb debt growth, drawing down the Gold and Foreign Exchange Contingency Reserve Account (GFECRA) by R150 billion over the next three years to help bring debt‑servicing costs to a peak of R1.21 billion per day by FY 2026/27.
Overall, the budget reflects a cautious approach to fiscal consolidation amid a geopolitically tense environment, sluggish growth, and rising expenditure pressures. The Treasury’s focus on narrowing the deficit, achieving a primary surplus, and stabilising the debt ratio is intended to mitigate medium‑term risks to South Africa’s sovereign rating, though the pace of reform remains slow.

Year‑on‑year growth was 0.6% and a six‑month comparison showed 0.7% growth. Seven of ten manufacturing divisions posted positive growth, with mining up 3.7% and manufacturing up 1.8%, each contributing 0.2 percentage points to GDP. Ackerman warned that without a turnaround in investment, budgets will remain tight and ratings agencies unconvinced, and said 'Until we see a turnaround in investment, budgets will remain tight and ratings agencies unconvinced.' He remains optimistic that the South African Reserve Bank has room to consider a rate cut later this month and in the coming months, amid weak growth, low inflation and the US Federal Reserve's rate‑cut cycle.

Gender equality is the goal, but how to get there? Case study of South Africa and Australia shows that context matters
The World Economic Forum’s Global Gender Gap Report 2023 projects that it will take an estimated 131 years for the world to reach gender parity – defined as equal access, opportunities and outcomes for women and men across economic, political, educational and health dimensions – and most of us will never see it happen.  Gender parity matters because women comprise more than half of the global population, and excluding them from full participation imposes economic and social costs; closing the gap is therefore a condition for sustained growth, innovation and societal wellbeing.
A research team of economists compared South Africa and Australia to illustrate how context shapes gender‑equality policies.  South Africa follows an equity‑based approach rooted in post‑apartheid redress, with laws on gender‑based violence, reproductive rights, employment equity and affirmative action.  In contrast, Australia pursues an equality‑focused strategy that emphasizes workplace reforms, reporting and institutional mechanisms such as the Sex Discrimination Act, the Workplace Gender Equality Act, a Women’s Budget Statement and a Women’s Economic Equality Taskforce.
Key metrics highlight divergent challenges: 97.3 % of South Africans hold at least one gender bias (the highest rate globally), and women’s labour‑force participation was 35.4 % in 2022 versus 59.1 % in Australia.  South Africa ranked 33rd and Australia 13th in the Global Gender Gap Index 2025, yet South Africa’s participation gap is larger and its cultural norms remain more entrenched.  Australia has made measurable progress in pay and leadership representation but still struggles with wage gaps and cultural change.
The study concludes that gender equality advances through different pathways shaped by each country’s social, historical and institutional context, and that no universal model can address such diverse realities.  Tailored, evidence‑based policies are therefore the best hope to close the gap in less than 131 years.
